Markets · Week Ending 21 August 2026
Last Week's Wrap

The week had two spines pulling against each other: a long end at nineteen-year highs and a Treasury that blinked. The 30-year printed its highest yield since 2007 mid-week — 5.31% Monday, 5.34% Tuesday — on the largest monthly federal deficit since March 2021 and an inflation rate that has now run above target for five straight years. Wednesday, Treasury doubled the maximum size of its long-end buybacks from $2bn to $4bn per operation. It bought exactly one session. By Friday the 30-year had settled near 5.276%, up 3.9bp on the day, the 10-year around 4.74% and the 2-year at 4.24%. The detail that keeps getting lost: the enlarged operations don't begin until 9 September. Nothing structural has changed, and the market has already looked through the announcement twice.

Friday refused to confirm Thursday's panic. The flash composite PMI printed 56.0, the fastest US business growth since April 2022, with services at 56.8, a twenty-month high, hiring at its best pace in over a year and price pressures at multi-month lows. The tape bought it: S&P 500 +0.43% to 7,674.37, Dow +517.80 points or +0.98% to 53,277.01, Nasdaq Composite +113.29 to 26,180.45, Russell 2000 +0.9% to 3,017.87, VIX back to 15.13. None of it rescued the week — the S&P still finished roughly 1.4% lower and the Nasdaq roughly 2% lower. Friday bought one session, not the trend.

Where the relief liquidity actually went is the week's real signal. Bitcoin closed Friday +6% at $76,943.90 after trading above $77,000, roughly 20% higher across the seventy-two hours around the buyback; gold reached $4,617.50, its best since mid-May, up more than 2% on Friday alone. Both moved explicitly on US-debt headlines, the same week the national debt crossed $40 trillion. Note the correlation break: gold rallied with real yields backing up, not against them, which is not how it traded in July. A debt manager capping the price of its own liabilities while gold and bitcoin run is not a bond market being calmed — it is a sovereign being repriced, and the liquidity meant to settle the market that funds the government went instead to the two assets with no sovereign counterparty at all.

Underneath the index, the dispersion was violent and one-directional. Big Tech fell more than 3% on the week and Meta around 7% across five sessions — the most rate-sensitive, highest-multiple names paid for the long end's move while the headline recovered on Friday. The counterweight was Micron, re-rating toward $967 on a fiscal Q3 that did more than $41bn of revenue, up around 340% year-on-year, with EPS above $25 against $1.91 a year ago. The index level and the index leadership diverged all week, and it is the leadership telling the truth about the discount rate.

The weekend then added two trade shocks. US-Canada talks collapsed late Friday night and 50% tariffs took effect at midnight on roughly $20bn of Canadian goods — building materials, liquor, certain clothing, hockey sticks. Mark Carney called the tariffs "a miscalculation," blamed "uneconomic" and "unfair" American demands, and set Canadian retaliation for 8 September on steel, dairy, agricultural equipment and pulp and paper. On the second front, the sixty-day US-Iran memorandum window expired 17 August with no deal, Trump declared on 19 August "the most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country," and Bessent promised the toughest-ever sanctions on Tehran. China answered by ordering its independent Shandong refiners to keep buying Iranian crude in open defiance. Brent posted a second consecutive weekly gain of about 6% to just under $94, WTI around $87.

The crosses and the credit market close the picture, and they disagree. The dollar index fell about 0.83% on the week to 98.82 — on the fiscal story, not the rate differential — lifting the euro 0.98% to 1.1692, a three-month high, the pound to 1.3641 and the yen to about 158.9, where Japanese July core inflation at 1.8% y/y firmed the case for a September BOJ hike. Europe closed Friday green (DAX +0.59% to 26,136.56, FTSE 100 +0.64% to 10,816.56, CAC 40 +0.37% to 8,484.43, Bund at 3.26%), while the Nikkei fell 0.30% to 66,016.36 on yield sensitivity and Hong Kong and Korea firmed on better China-demand signals. And then the market that didn't move at all: US high-yield spreads sat near 271–281bp, inside the richest decile of their own history against a ~450bp long-run median — through a 2007 high in the long bond, a hawkish chair five days from the podium and two live tariff fronts.

5 Points Looking Ahead to This Week
  1. Rates: Warsh's Jackson Hole debut lands on the same morning as core PCE. The symposium runs 27–29 August on financial innovation; the new chair's first keynote is Friday morning, the same morning July core PCE prints, nineteen days before the 16 September FOMC. He has said he'd step back from "near-sighted debates" and that the Fed is "not constrained by market prices." With a September cut priced at zero and a hike near 30%, the surprise that repositions this market is hawkish, not dovish. The real tell is whether he engages with the long end at all — a chair who talks about fiscal dominance moves the 30-year far more than one who talks about September.
  2. Equities: NVIDIA on Wednesday night is the entire tape. It reports after the close against consensus in the $92–94bn revenue range and $2.09–2.13 EPS depending on the compiler, Data Center estimated above $85bn, a four-for-four beat streak and ~95% implied odds of another. At that positioning the beat is required, not optional — and China DC-compute revenue is excluded from guidance entirely, sitting on exactly the fault line Beijing lit this week. Salesforce, CrowdStrike, Synopsys, Marvell, HP and Dell report into the same window, so a disappointment doesn't stay in one name.
  3. The long end versus the 2007 peak, with the buyback still three weeks out. The 30-year near 5.28% sits ~16bp below the 5.44% pre-crisis high, the doubled operations don't start until 9 September, and the market has already looked through the announcement. A rebuild through 5.34% before the operations have even begun would be the market saying plainly that $4bn is not the marginal buyer.
  4. Credit: the market that hasn't moved has the least room. High yield inside the richest decile of its own history has ignored everything the long end has done. Watch the two lines against each other — a 30-year through 5.44% with no sympathy widening says credit is mispricing a genuine repricing event; spreads through 300bp on the same catalyst says the catch-up is live. The gap is where the week's real risk sits, not in the index headline.
  5. FX and commodities: oil is now a policy instrument. Brent near $94 after two 6% weekly gains is driven by a sanctions campaign rather than by barrels, so the swing factor is enforcement against Iran's buyers — and China has already said it won't comply. Alongside it, a dollar at 98.8 that fell on the fiscal story rather than the differential, the yen into a live September BOJ call, and the 8 September Canadian retaliation.

Also on the calendar: Jackson Hole opens (Thu), NVIDIA and the AI-earnings wall (Wed AMC), revised Q2 GDP (Thu), July core PCE and Warsh's keynote (Fri), Canadian retaliation (8 Sep).

Bull / Base / Bear: The Week Ahead

Bull (~25%). Warsh keeps to structural themes and sounds untroubled by the long end, core PCE cooperates, and NVIDIA beats large enough to re-rate the complex rather than merely clear the bar. The 30-year pulls back from its 2007 test as term-premium fears ease, the Canada and Iran threads plateau, and credit keeps its tights because growth really is as good as a 56.0 composite says. Equities recover the week's losses into month-end.

Base (~45%). Nothing resolves. Warsh says little, by design and on the record, and core PCE prints close enough to consensus to settle nothing. NVIDIA beats by the usual 2–4% and the stock doesn't carry the index. The 30-year oscillates in a 5.15–5.35% band while the buyback is anticipated but not yet operating. High yield starts to lag modestly. Dispersion beats direction, the curve stays steep, the long end sets the ceiling on the multiple.

Bear (~30%). Warsh delivers explicit hike-optionality alongside a hot core PCE, the 30-year takes out 5.44%, and high yield finally snaps wider off its priced-for-perfection tights. China escalates its sanctions defiance into broader retaliation that clouds Wednesday's NVIDIA read, and the 8 September Canadian retaliation stops looking negotiable. The de-rating accelerates as the discount rate, not earnings, sets the multiple — and the dollar falls with yields rising, the combination that has no comfortable hedge except gold.

The Book — Honest Scoreboard

Three positions open, ten closed. MM-2026-013, short the US 2-year yield, was closed discretionarily on 23 August at −1.87% after 76 days: its catalyst was a dovish June FOMC repricing that resolved two months ago, and holding a rate-cut expression into a zero-cut, 30%-hike setup was indefensible. The closed ledger now reads one win in ten — the SPX put spread hedge returned +28.57%; Broadcom, two Brent structures, the Hormuz call spread, the DAX/Nasdaq ratio, gold, short USD/JPY, long 10-year duration and now the 2-year all came off negative. A 10% hit rate and an average closed trade of −13.87% are the numbers, and the pattern hasn't changed: nearly every loss was a reactive position sized for a geopolitical or Fed resolution the tape then refused to deliver on schedule.

The open book is still one position wearing three names. The 2s10s steepener opened 1 June at +15bp is marked at +230.67% with the curve at 49.6bp. Short EUR/AUD is +1.01% and short EUR/USD is −0.66%, and neither has a live catalyst — EUR/USD's ECB event resolved in June, EUR/AUD has had nothing driving it for weeks.

Here is the part worth sitting with. Last week's note flagged that the open positions shared one implicit assumption about the front end. That assumption has now broken twice. The 2-year trade is closed for it. And look at the steepener: it came in from +246% to +230.67%, and 2s10s flattened from 51.9bp to 49.6bp, in the very week the long end printed a 2007 high. The flattening didn't come from the long end rallying — it came from the front end selling off on Friday's PMI. So the same front-end sensitivity that killed one position is now quietly taxing the one position carrying the entire book, in the exact macro environment the steepener was designed for. That isn't a directional problem. It's a construction problem.

Focus Idea of the Week

The idea follows directly from that construction problem, and it's a change of expression rather than a change of view: shift the long-end exposure from 2s10s to 2s30s. The thesis has always been fiscal supply and term premium, which live at the very long end; the 10-year leg imports front-end sensitivity that has now cost the book twice in a fortnight, once fatally. A 2s30s steepener isolates the part of the curve the thesis is actually about and stops the position being hostage to whether the next PMI lifts the 2-year. Take part of the +230% off in the process rather than defending the whole mark into two events that both act on the long end.

The second leg is the one regime expression the book doesn't own at all: protection on US high yield. Spreads in the richest decile of their own history, into a hawkish chair, a hot PMI, an NVIDIA print that has to clear ~95% implied beat odds and two live tariff fronts, is poor asymmetry in one direction and very good in the other. The honest caveat is that this is a catch-up trade with no catalyst of its own — if Warsh is data-dependent and NVIDIA beats cleanly, spreads stay tight and carry bleeds it — so size it as dated event protection into Friday, not as a conviction short, and let 5.44% on the 30-year be the trigger to add rather than the level to chase. The outcome to design against remains a bear flattening, in which the July dissenters get their hike and the 2-year catches the 30. That single scenario damages the steepener and the short EUR/USD leg together — precisely what happened in miniature this week, twice, as a warning.
