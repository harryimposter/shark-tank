## <b><u>Last Week's Wrap</u></b>

The week the Treasury blinked — and the market took the hint about the currency rather than the yield. Monday opened with the 30 year bond up almost six basis points at 5.31%, the highest since 2007, and Tuesday pushed it to 5.34%, a nineteen year high, on a July federal deficit that was the largest monthly total since March 2021 and an inflation rate that has now sat above the Fed's target for five straight years. This month's $25bn thirty year auction had already cleared at 5.216%, the highest for that auction since 2001. On Wednesday the Treasury responded: it doubled the maximum size of its long end liquidity support buybacks from $2bn to $4bn per operation across the 10-20 and 20-30 year sectors, effective 9 September through 4 November. The thirty year fell nine basis points to 5.196% and the ten year closed down over five at 4.647%.

The relief lasted one session. By Thursday yields had resumed their march higher, the Dow shed more than 700 points for its worst day since 29 July, the S&P 500 fell 0.87% to 7,641.16 and the Nasdaq dropped 1%. From Monday's close of 7,745.06 the S&P gave up 1.3% in four sessions and sits below the record it set earlier this month. What did hold were the two prices that matter for reading the intervention: the dollar, already at its lowest since early June on Monday as hike bets faded, extended its slide after the announcement, and gold jumped more than 4% on Wednesday to around $4,530, its highest since early June. A debt manager capping the price of its own liabilities, a currency that falls and a gold price that runs is not a bond rally — it is a repricing of the sovereign.

The front end said the same thing from the other direction. September hike odds have collapsed to roughly 30% from an 82.4% peak in late July, built on a July payroll print that shed 23,000 jobs against expectations of an 85,000 gain, a July CPI bang on consensus at 0.1% monthly and 3.4% annual with core at 2.5%, and July retail sales falling for the first time in nine months. On that data the two year should have rallied hard. It did not: it finished the week at 4.185%, three basis points *higher* than the Friday before, having touched 4.192% on Tuesday. Wednesday's July FOMC minutes explain part of it — the committee held at 3.50-3.75% on the most fractured vote in years, 9-3, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting for a quarter point hike, and officials recording the need to move if inflation does not cool. Warsh also used the meeting to float cutting the FOMC calendar from eight meetings to six, with no conclusion reached. Softer data, collapsing hike odds, and the front end sold off anyway.

The cleanest evidence that this was a fiscal week and not a monetary one is in the crosses. EUR/USD rose 1.20% to 1.1692 while EUR/AUD was flat at 1.6350, up 0.08%. The euro did nothing; the dollar did all of it, broadly, against everything. Our own curve position tells the same story: 2s10s ran to 55.2bp on Tuesday as the long end broke, collapsed to 48.8bp on Wednesday's buyback, and closed the week at 51.9bp, with the implied ten year at 4.70% — within a basis point of where the tape had it Friday morning.

Underneath the macro, corporate results were quietly excellent and quietly bifurcated. All five large caps that reported this week beat on EPS, but the composition matters. Keysight beat by 21.6% at $3.07 against $2.52 with revenue $74m ahead — its third consecutive beat above 20%. Analog Devices beat by 2.4% at $3.45 with revenue $4.02bn against $3.96bn, on trailing revenue growth of 29.8% and EPS growth of 83.2%. Nordson and Jack Henry beat on both lines. Deere beat EPS by 5.5% at $5.10 — and missed revenue at $10.999bn against $11.05bn expected, with trailing twelve month EPS down 14.6%. The instrumentation and analog names beat on demand; the industrial bellwether beat on cost. That is the AI capex economy and the real economy printing in the same week, and only one of them is growing its top line.

Commodities stayed hostage to the strait. Brent rose to $93.01 on Thursday, up 1.5% on the day, roughly a quarter above pre conflict levels. Crude flows through Hormuz have genuinely recovered — about 5m bpd in July against 4m in June and 1.6m in May — but transits are averaging only about ten a day, the lowest since 11 May, and a projectile struck a vessel exiting the strait this week, killing a crew member and bringing the toll to 17 mariners across 65 attacks on commercial shipping since the war began on 28 February. Trump called the strait "open and operating." Iran's stated conditions for reopening it — naval blockade lifted, sanctions lifted, US forces withdrawn, war reparations paid, frozen assets released — are not a list that closes quickly.

## <b><u>5 Points Looking Ahead to this Week</u></b>

1. **Jackson Hole: Warsh's debut, and he has already told you he will not tell you.** The symposium runs 27-29 August on the theme of financial innovation, with the new chair's first keynote on Friday morning, nineteen days before the 16 September FOMC. He said on 29 July that he would step back from "near sighted debates" to raise "big questions," and that the Fed is "not constrained by market prices." With a hike only ~30% priced and three of his colleagues having already dissented for one in July, the risk is asymmetric: the surprise that repositions this market is hawkish, not dovish. The real tell is whether he engages with the long end at all — a chair who talks about fiscal dominance moves the thirty year far more than one who talks about September.

2. **NVIDIA on Wednesday night is the entire equity tape.** A $5.25tn market cap reports after the close with consensus at $2.13 EPS on $93.6bn of revenue, 64 of 68 analysts at buy, and trailing revenue growth of 70.7%. The last four surprises were +2.1%, +2.0%, +3.6% and +4.3% — at this positioning the beat is required, not optional. It lands the same evening as Salesforce, CrowdStrike, Synopsys, Okta, HP and Nutanix, so a disappointment does not stay in one name. With the Nasdaq already the week's worst index on higher discount rates, this is the test of whether AI earnings can outrun the long bond.

3. **The buyback is a test, not a fix — and it does not start until 9 September.** Doubling operations to $4bn rearranges the maturity schedule; it retires no debt. Thursday already demonstrated the market can look straight through it. Watch the thirty year: a rebuild back above 5.34% before the operations have even begun would be the market stating plainly that $4bn is not the marginal buyer, and it would put the question of who funds the deficit at the centre of the Jackson Hole week.

4. **The dollar has become a fiscal instrument.** A currency at a two and a half month low while the front end is *selling off* is not a rate differential move. The differential argues the same way for now — roughly 84% odds of a September ECB hike against ~30% for the Fed — but the more important question is whether dollar weakness starts correlating with US yield *increases*, which is the emerging market signature. Gold up over 4% on a debt management announcement says part of the market is already trading it that way.

5. **Hormuz is the inflation input that keeps not resolving.** Brent near $93 with transits at their lowest since May is a supply story with no clearing mechanism, and it is what keeps the Fed's three dissenters loud and the long end nervous. Any genuine progress on the strait is the single most disinflationary event available to this tape; any further escalation lands directly on the part of the curve that is already at nineteen year highs.

## <b><u>Bull / Base / Bear: the week ahead</u></b>

**Bull (~25%).** Warsh keeps to structural themes and sounds untroubled by the long end, NVIDIA beats large enough to re-rate the complex rather than just clear the bar, and the buyback plus month end index extension buying pulls the thirty year back under 5.20%. The dollar's slide gets re-read as dovish rather than fiscal, and equities recover the week's losses into month end.

**Base (~45%).** Nothing resolves. Warsh says little, by design and on the record. NVIDIA beats by the usual 2-4% and the stock does not carry the index. The thirty year oscillates in a 5.15-5.35% band while the buyback is anticipated but not yet operating. Dispersion beats direction, the curve stays steep, and the tape chops with the long end setting the ceiling on the multiple.

**Bear (~30%).** Warsh reminds the market that three colleagues wanted to hike in July and that he is not constrained by market prices; the thirty year takes out 5.34% into the event; NVIDIA is a sell the news. The de-rating that began this week accelerates as the discount rate, not earnings, sets the multiple — and the dollar falls *with* yields rising, the combination that has no comfortable hedge except gold.

## <b><u>The book — honest scoreboard</u></b>

Four positions open, nine closed. The closed ledger reads one win against eight stops: the SPX put spread hedge returned +28.6%, and everything else — Broadcom, two Brent structures, the Hormuz call spread, the DAX/Nasdaq ratio, gold, short USD/JPY, long 10 year duration — was stopped out. An 11% hit rate on closed trades is the number, and the pattern in it is that every loss was a *reactive* trade positioned for a geopolitical or Fed resolution that the tape then refused to deliver on schedule.

The open book is carried entirely by one position. The 2s10s steepener opened on 1 June at +15bp is now +51.9bp, marked at +246%, and added 7.3 points this week alone; it is 82% of the way to its +60bp target. The other three are noise or worse: short EUR/AUD +0.61%, short EUR/USD -0.79% and short the US 2 year yield -0.55%, the latter two having given back 1.2 and 0.8 points respectively this week.

That last detail is the one worth sitting with. Short EUR/USD is a long dollar position and short the 2 year yield is a dovish position; on paper they hedge each other. This week they lost together, because the week delivered a falling dollar *and* rising front end yields simultaneously — the one corner of the macro space where both bleed. The book was not wrong on direction. It was positioned for a world where the dollar and US rates still move together, and that is precisely the assumption this week broke.

## <b><u>Focus idea of the week</u></b>

The book already owns the right expression of this regime, so the idea is discipline rather than direction. The steepener has been carried by fiscal supply, not by the Fed, and next week hands it two events that each act directly on the long end: Warsh at Jackson Hole and the run up to the Treasury's first enlarged buyback on 9 September. The trade is to take the structure down, not off — bank part of a +246% move and roll the remainder into a conditional steepener, payer spreads on the long end financed against the front, keeping the fiscal premium exposure while capping what a credible buyback or a hawkish chair can claw back.

The outcome to design against is not a flattening. It is a *bear* flattening, in which the July dissenters get their hike and the two year catches up to the ten. That single scenario damages the steepener, the short 2 year yield position and the short EUR/USD position at the same time — which is exactly what happened in miniature this week, at small size, as a warning. Three of four open positions share one implicit assumption about the front end. Sizing should reflect that they are one trade, not three.
